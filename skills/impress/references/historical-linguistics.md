# Historical linguistics and etymology

## Evaluator

An interested general reader at the surface and a skeptical historical linguist in the evidence layer.

## Governing principle

Lay-first, scholar-deep.

## Exemplar signals

- Begin with a clear, chronological word story.
- Represent form, language or stage, date range, meaning, and relation type explicitly.
- Label borrowing, inheritance, derivation, compounding, influence, cognacy, and uncertainty distinctly.
- Separate phonological change from semantic change.
- Preserve provenance at the claim or relationship level.
- Show disagreement and missing evidence without flattening the public narrative.
- Use corpus evidence for usage claims, never as proof of origin.

## Expert-noticed distinctions

- Attested versus reconstructed forms.
- Cognacy versus resemblance.
- Inheritance versus borrowing.
- Language-family ancestry versus direct lexical descent.
- Colexification versus polysemy, semantic similarity, or cognacy.
- Database inclusion versus scholarly consensus.
- A historical stage versus its modern namesake.

## Anti-patterns

- Unlabeled arrows where relation type matters.
- Exact dates when only ranges are supported.
- Phonetic or orthographic similarity presented as historical evidence.
- Giant graphs that obscure source and direction.
- Generated narrative that exceeds structured evidence.

## Evidence standard

Retain source dataset, original identifier or record, license, normalization method, evidential status, and build version for consequential claims.

## Acceptance checks

- A first-time reader understands the journey.
- One evidence-based detail is genuinely memorable.
- An expert can inspect the source of every consequential relation.
- Uncertainty is visible without becoming the whole story.
